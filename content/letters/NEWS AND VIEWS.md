# NEWS AND VIEWS

## 🔗 קישורים ומידע ארכיוני

Documents of Our Times: Yosef Zvi Rimon

By Pinhas H. Peli. Appreciation of Remarkable Personality.

By DR. S. MARGOSHES

(From an Article by Pinhas H. Peli in \"Iggeret Lagolah,\" Jerusalem.)

He belongs to no school of poets. His poetry not conform to any of the kinds of poetry known to us in either modern or ancient Hebrew literature; he stands alone, unique in his time. Those who wished to discover his roots in the great religious poetry of the Middle Ages and pointed out his kinship with Ibn Gabirol and Yehuda HaLevi were only partially justified.

Without doubt he is a religious poet; he expresses in his poetry a closeness to God and a longing for holiness unparalleled in his generation. He sings of himself: \"A Hebrew mark is on my gates, the name of God is engraved on my flag,\" and many of his religious hymns deserve to be included in the Sidur, like the lyrics of the great poets of the Middle Ages.

But it must be pointed out before we place him with Ibn Gabirol and HaLevi, that Rimon is also a modern poet par excellence, in mode of expression and in inner being. All the torments of the modern man, all his agony and doubts, the deep chasm which has opened at his feet---all these are seen clearly, without evasion, in the poetry of Yosef Tsvi Rimon. His way to God is not the firm road paved by generations, but rather one of his own making. Created out of the many and difficult sufferings of the soul, the road rises and falls until it finds itself peaceful and quiet, close to the Shekhina.

Yosef Tsvi Rimon landed at Jaffa Port in 1909 from a small town in Poland, a young man of twenty, unassuming and shy, with deep black eyes. In his home village he had studied Torah in a Yeshiva and longed for Eretz Israel. He had already written several poems and his few readers were struck by the sense of loneliness conveyed in the short, wavering lines. When he reached the Holy Land, his delight was unbounded. In his home village he had studied Torah in a Yeshiva and longed for Eretz Israel. He had already written several poems and his few readers were struck by the sense of loneliness conveyed in the short, wavering lines. When he reached the Holy Land, his delight was unbounded. He would stand for hours on the Jaffa shore, listening to the noise the waves made dashing against the dunes. Years later we can hear the echo of that murmur in his poems, but in the meantime he alone hears them, the young dreamer, filled with turbulence and longings. Here in Jaffa he met the man who was then rabbi of the city, and later to be the Chief Rabbi of Eretz Israel, the rabbi-poet-philosopher, HaRav Avraham Yitshak Kook. The brilliance of HaRav Kook who was then at the height of his powers, took possession of the young man he entered the Yeshiva of the Rav, thirstily drank in each syllable which fell from his lips; and the Rav too saw before him a young man of great soul. The two of them would take long walks together, either in lively conversation or in the heavy silence of meditation. Years later, the poet declared that in those same conversations \"the Rav rolled back the seal from the fountain of his poetry.\"

And then it was as if the fountain of his poetry suddenly flowed afresh, or as if a taut-stringed violin was hidden in his soul and every wind that blew played a song on it. He wrote hundreds of poems; long ones and short ones. He wrote them on every bit of paper he could lay his hands on; he filled notebook upon notebook with them, which he handed afterwards to his friends, to anyone he chanced to meet. Only a few saw print in various newspapers, in ephemeral magazines. Of the rest the greater part was lost, and only a smaller part was kept by friends and acquaintances. He himself paid no attention to his poems; he was content with writing them; once they were down on paper, he had no more to do with them; already he sought new poems, a new poetry. For this is the most outstanding characteristic of the poetry of Yosef Tsvi Rimon: the unquenchable thirst for the new.

And the thirst of Rimon is the thirst of the poet of the Psalms, \"My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God.\" Honestly and simply he sings:

These wanderings and these longings of which Rimon sang were part not only of his poems, but of his life. For many years he could not stay in the same place for two days running. One would meet him in the mountains of Jerusalem, leaping from rock to rock; or see him in the fields of the Sharon, among the flower beds, or in the scented citrus groves of Judea. He would also roam the shores of the Mediterranean, from where he would go by foot to the Kinneret, bathe himself in its waters and from there go up to the mountains of Galilee, and pass the night in the ancient caves among the olive trees --- taking into his soul the scenes of our land, which play with a thousand lights and reflections in every one of his poems. And he would wait, expecting at every moment to hear the voice of the Creator speaking to him; he longed for a sign, and his longing burned in him until it seemed to him that \"now I shall die, be consumed in my longings.\"

But his soul was satisfied at last, and his stormy spirit anchored in a safe harbor. The poet did not die of his longings and when he was already \"beyond despair\" he found what his soul sought and then he burst forth in a tremendous poetry which sang simply, like an innocent boy who has attained his desire after many efforts. Then his poetry found its own depths.

In the year 1921 the poet was wandering, as was his custom, in the mountains of Galilee, when he met a gang of Arab bandits who tortured him. This experience left its mark on his poetry which cried out against the injustice of man to man. The storm in his heart grew and his poetry rose in strength, mingling hope and despair.

It was when a part of his poems was published in a book under the title of Ketarim (\"Crowns\") in 1944 by \"Am Oved\" the Histadrut publishing house, that Rimon, already nearing sixty, was revealed in his full brilliance and acclaimed as one of the most important of modern Hebrew poets. Well-known critics who until then had been unable to evaluate his poetry, which had been scattered in little-known journals and forgotten periodicals, praised him. Teachers introduced his poems into the schools and the figure of Rimon strongly influenced a rising generation of religious poets. The latter group put out a large collection of his poetry, two years ago entitled Emunim.

This year Rimon\'s fifty years of creative activity are being celebrated. The event is being marked by receptions in cities and towns by the Organization of Hebrew Writers in Israel, the Government Department of Education and Culture and the Rav Kook Foundation in Jerusalem. The poet was invited to the home of President Ben-Zvi, where he was presented with a special token of recognition in the name of the State of Israel: \"To one of her sons seeking God in storm and in song.\"

At these receptions one can see the seventy-year-old poet, dressed in a long coat and a black cloth hat after the manner of the Jerusalemites, his head adorned with a beard and sidelocks, his voice quiet and peaceful his face tired and pale, only his huge eyes still exerting their mysterious spell. In the twilight of his days, the poet pours forth his soul in a quiet voice, because it has returned from its wanderings. He now lives in the Yad Eliyahu quarter near Tel Aviv, and every day he teaches a lesson in Tanakh with the commentary of Rashi before two minyanim of elders who gather daily in the synagogue of the neighborhood.
